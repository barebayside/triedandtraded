Methodology

How we test

A verdict is only worth as much as the process behind it. Here is ours, including the parts that limit what we can honestly claim.

Every test, same six steps

  1. Take the claim as it's actually made

    We test the strategy the way its advocates describe it — their deltas, their tenors, their exit rules. Testing a straw-man version and declaring it broken proves nothing. If the claim is vague, we test the most common interpretation and say so.

  2. Fix the rules before the run

    Entry, exit, position size, and what happens on assignment are written down first. This is the step that stops a test drifting into an exercise in finding the settings that look best.

  3. Run it across everything we have

    Not a favourable window. The full period, including the regimes where the strategy was never going to work. A strategy that only survives when you choose the years hasn't survived.

  4. Judge on expectancy, never win rate

    Average profit per trade after losses are counted at full size. A strategy can win nine times in ten and still lose money — that is the single most common way a bad strategy passes for a good one.

  5. Look for the condition

    Most strategies aren't good or bad, they're good inside a boundary. We look for the delta, tenor, or regime where the answer flips, and name it. "Conditional" is a real verdict, not a hedge.

  6. Publish the limits with the result

    Every test says what it can't tell you — before anyone asks. If we wouldn't be comfortable with a sceptic reading the caveats first, the test isn't ready.

Why we bother

The argument, in two characters
The bull

Someone says it works

"Sell puts on names you'd happily own. You either keep the premium or you buy a stock you wanted anyway. It can't really go wrong."

Every strategy arrives already believed. It comes with a story that makes sense, a name people recognise, and someone confident explaining it. None of that is evidence, and none of it is a reason to dismiss it either.

The bear

Someone says it doesn't

"It wins nine times in ten and the tenth takes back everything. You're picking up pennies."

The objection usually sounds just as reasonable. Both sides argue from stories and from the handful of trades they remember. Neither has counted.

The ferret

So we go and count

"Neither of you has run it. I have."

That is the entire job of this site. Not to have an opinion about a strategy, but to run it across everything we have and publish what came out — including when the answer is boring, and including when it makes our own software look bad.

What a verdict means

Three outcomes, no others
Holds

Positive expectancy across the full period, and it survives reasonable changes to the assumptions. Not a recommendation — a statement that the claim stood up.

Conditional

Works inside a stated boundary and fails outside it. The boundary is always named. Most honest strategies land here.

Fails

Negative expectancy, or positive only under assumptions no one could actually trade. Often paired with a high win rate — that's usually the point.

What a backtest is

A backtest is a record of what a set of rules would have produced against historical prices. It is evidence, not proof. It tells you whether a claim has ever been true, which is a much lower bar than whether it will be true — and a much higher bar than most strategy advice clears.

The four things we can't fix

  • Fills. Historical quotes are not the price you'd have got. We model this conservatively and say how.
  • Liquidity. A strategy that works on paper in thin options may be untradeable at size. Where that's a risk, we flag it.
  • Regime coverage. The sample contains the markets that happened, not the ones that could.
  • Your execution. Every result assumes the rules were followed exactly. Almost nobody does that.
What we will never do

We will not publish a figure that didn't come out of a run. We will not quietly change parameters until a strategy passes. We will not sell a strategy we graded, and we take no payment from anyone whose strategy we test. If a test makes our own software look bad, it gets published anyway.

Reproducibility

Every test publishes its parameters. Anyone with the same data and the same rules should reach the same verdict — and if you run it and don't, we want to hear about it.
