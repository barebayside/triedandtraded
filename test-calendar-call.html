The Archive  /  Test No. 1

The calendar call, 4,937 times

Sell the near expiry, buy the far one, let time do the work. It is the trade people describe as collecting rent. We ran it nearly five thousand times on prices somebody actually quoted.

Published 22 Aug 2026 Period tested Feb 2024 – Jun 2026 Trades 4,937 Data QuantConnect daily chains
Fails

The verdict

It made money — and 49 of the 4,937 trades were 96% of the profit. Take away the two best names out of nine and the whole thing loses. That is not an edge you can run; it is a lottery with a positive average.

The claim

The case for a calendar spread is a real one, and it goes like this.

  • Two legs, one strike. Sell the near-dated call, buy the far-dated call at the same strike.
  • The near one dies faster. Time decay accelerates as expiry closes, so the option you sold loses value quicker than the one you own.
  • You keep the difference. If the stock sits still, that gap is your profit.
  • The loss is capped. You pay a debit to open it, and the debit is the most you can lose.

Every one of those statements is true. None of them tells you whether the trade makes money, because none of them is about what the stock actually does next.

How we tested it

The specification was fixed before the run, and every value the software would otherwise have chosen for us is named here.

  • No entry filter at all. The trade opens on every day the chain allows it. We are testing the structure, not a signal.
  • No exit rule. Every trade runs to the near expiry. That is the baseline every other exit gets measured against.
  • Nine names, one window. No name was chosen after seeing its result.
Test parameters
UniverseAAPL · AMZN · BA · CVX · GOOGL · GS · QQQ · SMH · UNH
StructureLong calendar call — sell near, buy far, same strike
Entry delta0.25 – 0.35 on the long leg
Days to expiryShort leg 8–16 · long leg 25–35
Exit ruleHeld to the near expiry — no target, no stop
Minimum size1 contract
FillsMid, moved a quarter of the spread against us — at entry and at every daily mark
Period1 Feb 2024 – 30 Jun 2026
Prices41,566 daily marks, 41,459 of them (99.7%) off a real two-sided quote

That last row is the one that matters most, and it is the reason this test exists at all.

  • A model will price anything. Ask it for a value on a day nobody was quoting and it returns a confident number.
  • We record when there was no quote. A missing bid is stored as missing, so we know which marks were real and which were guessed.
  • An earlier version of this run was wrong about that, and the correction cost it five sixths of its result. See what this does not tell you.

Two things in that panel disagree with each other, and the disagreement is the whole article.

  • The average trade makes $12.83.
  • The median trade loses $23.00. More than half of them lose. The average is being carried by something.

Win rate is not the answer

A strategy can win most of the time and lose money, and it can lose most of the time and make money. Expectancy is the only one of the two that decides anything.

  • This one loses most of the time — 46.8% of trades win.
  • And still averages a profit — because the winners are far bigger than the losers.
  • Best trade: +$1,920. Worst: −$1,426. The tail on the upside is longer, and that is where the entire result lives.

So we asked how much of the profit came from how few trades.

Total profit, 4,937 trades+$63,338
From the best 1% (49 trades)+$61,068 — 96% of it
From the best 5% (246 trades)+$182,004 — 287% of it
Median trade−$23.00
  • Forty-nine trades are the strategy. The other 4,888 net out to +$2,270 between them, or 46 cents each.
  • The best 5% make nearly three times the total profit, which means everything outside the top 5% loses roughly two-thirds of it back.

Miss the 49 and you did not trade a worse version of this strategy. You traded a losing one.

Is it the structure, or is it the names?

A pooled average survives one name carrying it, and nothing in the pooled figure says so. So we took the best names out, one at a time.

What it does to an actual account

Every figure above is per trade. An account has to find the money for each one and hold it until the trade closes, so we ran the same trades through three account sizes.

$25,000Took 4,431 of 4,937 · +$24,963 · +$5.63 a trade · 79.3% drawdown
$50,000Took all 4,937 · +$63,338 · +$12.83 a trade · 54.0% drawdown
$100,000Took all 4,937 · +$63,338 · +$12.83 a trade · 35.8% drawdown
  • Under $50,000 the account runs out of money before the strategy runs out of signals — 506 trades declined for cash, and the per-trade average halves.
  • The drawdown is the number to sit with. Even funded properly, this took the account down 35.8% along the way to that profit.
  • Nine names at once is what makes it expensive. The signals arrive together, because they are all driven by the calendar and not by the stocks.

Did it hold up out of sample?

It was not tested out of sample, and we are not going to imply otherwise.

  • The hold-out years are 2015, 2019 and 2023. They are sealed and have never been looked at.
  • This test does not reach them. It runs from February 2024, so there is no out-of-sample result to report.
  • The three years it does cover are one regime, and they agree with each other — which proves less than it looks like it proves.
2024 (1,857 trades)47.1% win · +$3.59 a trade
2025 (2,097 trades)47.0% win · +$17.45 a trade

A verdict of Holds on this site requires an out-of-sample result that stands on its own. This test cannot earn one yet.

The condition that changes the answer

Most strategies are good inside a boundary and bad outside it. This one has a boundary, and it is an awkward one.

  • The condition is the name. On SMH it made $75 a trade; on UNH it lost $46.
  • That is not a rule you can apply in advance. You only know which names worked after they worked.
  • We also tested a trigger — entering only after a higher high off a horizontal level. It picked 212 trades and averaged $31.06, two and a half times better per trade.
  • And it made the account $18,378 worse, because it declined 4,725 trades to get there. Better per trade and worse for the account is not a contradiction; it is the difference between a ranking and a result.
What this test does not tell you
  • One regime, one window. Feb 2024 to Jun 2026, and no sealed year touched.
  • Nine names. Large, liquid US names. Nothing here says anything about a small cap.
  • No commissions. Fills are mid less a quarter of the spread, which is a real cost and the larger one, but brokerage is not modelled.
  • The final mark on each trade is settled, not quoted. The expiring leg is settled by arithmetic; the surviving leg is modelled, and the model prices it about 5% of mid above the market.
  • An earlier version of this run was wrong. It was pricing trades on days the market was shut — a model filling in where there was no quote at all. Corrected, the same 4,937 trades kept their entry dates and lost five sixths of their profit. The number on this page is the corrected one.

What we'd take from this

  • Do not run this as an income trade. The story is that it pays you rent while the stock sits still. The middle trade loses $23.
  • If you run it anyway, size for the tail. Your result is 1% of your trades. Stopping early because a run of losses feels wrong is how you miss them.
  • Fund it properly or do not fund it. Under $50,000 across nine names the account is the constraint, not the strategy.
  • The next version to test is not a better entry — it is a shorter far leg. The gap between 8–16 and 25–35 days is what sets how much the trade is a bet on time and how much it is a bet on the stock.

We tested the structure. The structure is not what made the money.

Ferret: nothing here settles whether the calendar call works. It settles that this specification, on these nine names, in this window, did not — and that any test which reports only a pooled average would have called it a win. The next one names the far leg's tenor before it runs, and reports the per-name table whether or not it flatters us.

You are selling time to somebody else. The near option decays faster than the far one. That gap is the trade.

Common questions

Asked the way people type them

Does a calendar spread actually make money?
Over 4,937 trades on nine large US names between February 2024 and June 2026 it averaged +$12.83 a trade. 96% of that came from 49 trades, and removing the two best names turned the total negative.

What is the win rate on a calendar call?
46.8% in this test. The median trade lost $23, so more than half of them lose and the average is carried by a small number of large winners.

How much capital do you need to run it?
Across nine names, under $50,000 the account declines trades it cannot fund — 506 of 4,937 at $25,000 — and the per-trade average halves.

Is a calendar spread a low-risk trade?
The most you can lose on one is the debit you paid. Run as a book, this test drew the account down 35.8% at $100,000 and 79.3% at $25,000.
