The bull put spread

  • Sell a put below the price, buy a further one below that, and keep the difference.
  • The trade people reach for when they are mildly optimistic — and do not want punishing for being early.

What it is

  • Two legs. Same stock, same expiry.
  • You sell a put. That is the obligation, and the income.
  • You buy a cheaper put underneath it. That is the cap.
  • The difference between the two premiums is paid to you the moment you open the trade.

What you are actually agreeing to

  • You are paid to open it.
  • You keep the payment if the stock stays above the strike you sold.
  • The put you bought caps what you can lose — it is not there to make money, it is there to stop the loss running.
  • That cap is what turns an open-ended obligation into a number you can see before you enter.

Reading it back

  • One expiry, 16 October 2026 — 45 days away. Both legs share it, which is what makes this a vertical.
  • Sell the 95 put at 25 delta for $1.35. That is the income and the obligation.
  • Buy the 90 put at 10 delta for $0.41. That is the cap, and what it costs.
  • Net $0.93 a share, $93 the contract, paid to you on the day you open.

On delta, since it is written two ways

  • The model gives a put a negative delta — -0.25 on the leg you sold.
  • Traders drop the sign and the decimal and say “25 delta”.
  • Same number. The table shows both so neither reader is lost.
  • Selling a put means you are long that delta — the minus signs cancel, which is why the net position is bullish.

The argument

Bull

Makes the claim

This is my kind of trade. I don’t have to be right about direction — only about how far it won’t fall. The stock can rise, sit still, or drift down a little, and I still keep the lot.

Bear

Doubts it

You are being paid rent, and rent is paid for a reason. That credit is what somebody thinks it is worth to be able to hand you the stock at 95. Collect it forty times, give it back twice, and you have gone backwards.

Ferret

Settles it

Neither of you has said anything I can count. Which delta, which tenor, how wide, what minimum credit — and what closes it. Name those and I will go and measure. Until then you are both describing a mood.

The shape of it

How to read it

  • The thick line is the payoff at expiry.
  • The three faint lines are the same position with 45, 21 and 7 days left to run.
  • The gap between them is the trade.

What the gap tells you

  • At 45 days the line is a soft curve. Almost nothing is decided.
  • By 7 days it has hardened into the shape everyone recognises.
  • The money is now made or lost across a much narrower band of price.
  • Nothing about the position changed. Only the time left did.

Where you actually enter

The chart above is drawn at expiry, and every chart like it has the same flaw: the bend sits near the money, so the trade looks like it is already on the edge. It is not.

  • On the day you open it, the stock is a long way above the strike.
  • The bend is not where you are standing. It is where you would be after a serious move against you.
  • And on day one the line through your position is smooth — there is no bend there at all yet.
Day one · 21 delta short

Sell the 94 put, buy the 89 · 21Δ · 45 days · $78 credit
Priced at 25% implied volatility and a 4% rate, on a $100 stock.

What this one is showing

  • You enter at 100, with the short strike at 94. That is 6% of room before the strike is even touched.
  • The solid line is day one. Where you are standing it is almost flat. Nothing is decided.
  • The dashed line is expiry. That is the shape everyone draws — and it is the only day it is ever true.
  • The bend is over on the left, six per cent away. You are nowhere near it.

And the bend is worse than it looks

Take the stock down to the short strike and see what the same price is worth on two different days.

Stock atDay oneAt expiry
100 — where you entered$0$+78
94 — at the short strike$-109$+78
  • At expiry, the stock sitting exactly on the strike is a full win — $+78, the whole credit.
  • On day one the same price is $-109 — more than the entire credit, already gone.
  • So by the time price reaches the bend, you are not at the edge of the trade. You are most of the way through it.

The hockey stick is a picture of one day out of forty-five. Every other day you are on the smooth line, and the smooth line moves against you long before the bend arrives.

What changes if you move the strike

  • Same stock, same expiry, same $5 width.
  • The only thing that moves is which strike you sell.

Cushion is how far the stock can fall before the trade loses money — spot to breakeven.

  • Credit falls from $213 to $52.
  • Risk climbs from $287 to $448, because the width is fixed and the credit comes out of it.
  • Risk to reward goes from 1.4 : 1 to 8.6 : 1.
  • The far strike is not the safe one. It is the one that has to win far more often to break even.

Which is why delta is not a probability

  • A 15 delta short strike gets described as a 15% chance of finishing in the money.
  • It is a rough stand-in, not the thing itself.
  • And it says nothing about how much you lose when it happens — which is the number that just got bigger.
  • Whether the extra room pays for the worse ratio is a question we can test. It is on the list.

Different ways to set it up

There is no such thing as the bull put spread. There is a family of them, and two dials decide which one you are holding.

  • How long you give it — the tenor.
  • How far out you sell — the delta.
  • Every panel below is the same $5-wide spread on the same $100 stock. Only those two things change.

The surprise in that table

  • 20 delta is not a distance. At 7 days it puts the strike 3% below the stock. At 90 days the same 20 delta is 8% below.
  • Time is what buys the distance. The delta number does not move; the room underneath it goes from 3% to 8%.
  • And the payoff is not close. $33 at 7 days against $91 at 90 — but 14 : 1 against 4.5 : 1.
  • Look at the panels: the short-dated one has almost no gap between its day-one line and its expiry line. There is no time left to be wrong in.

  • Far out (15Δ) the shape slides left and flattens. 8% of room, but only $52.

The two dials pull against each other. Tenor buys you distance and charges you time. Delta buys you distance and charges you money.

Which is the point of testing it

  • Every one of these is called a bull put spread.
  • They are not the same trade, and they will not produce the same result.
  • Nobody publishing a win rate for “the bull put spread” has told you which of these they ran.
  • That is what a specification is for, and it is why we name all four numbers before a test starts.

What the Greeks are doing

  • Four questions about the same position, and a fifth that barely applies here.
  • Every structure answers them differently.
  • The answers are what the structure is. The name is only a label on top.
GreekHereThe question it answers
DeltapositiveWhich way you need the stock to go
ThetapositiveWhat the passing of time does to you
VeganegativeWhat a change in fear does to you
GammanegativeHow fast your delta turns against you
RhosmallWhat a change in interest rates does to you

Delta

  • The put you sold is the bigger of the two legs.
  • So the position leans bullish whether you meant it to or not.
  • Net +15 delta — roughly a 15-share bet that the stock does not fall far.

Theta

  • You sold more premium than you bought.
  • Every day that passes without movement pays you.
  • This is the number the whole strategy gets sold on — and on its own it is true.

Vega

  • You are short volatility.
  • If implied volatility rises, the spread costs more to buy back.
  • That happens whether or not the stock has moved a cent.

Gamma

  • This is the one that does the damage.
  • Short gamma means the position gets more bullish as the stock falls.
  • You are getting longer into the decline — at exactly the moment you would rather be getting shorter.

Rho is the fifth, and it only asks what happens if interest rates move. At 45 days that is worth about $1.97 for a full one per cent shift — barely a day of theta. It matters on long-dated legs, not here.

Theta and gamma are one thing

This is the part that decides whether the strategy works, and it is almost never put plainly.

You cannot be paid theta without being short gamma. They are not two features of the trade. They are the rent and the risk on one lease.

Which is what DTE actually sets

  • Days to expiry is not one dial among several.
  • It is the dial that moves both numbers at once — in the same direction.
  • Shorter tenor: more income per day, more violence per dollar.
  • Longer tenor: less of both.
  • There is no setting that hands you one without the other.

So the 21-day rule is not about time

  • The widely repeated rule says close at 21 days.
  • It is not really a rule about time. It is a rule about gamma wearing a time rule’s clothes.
  • Leave before the second number runs away from you.
  • Whether it earns its keep is a question we can test — and it is on the list.

The trap

Every structure has one thing that catches people. On this one it is the stop.

A stop here is a percentage of what you staked — not of what you can lose.

  • You took in $93.
  • You can lose $407.
  • A two hundred per cent stop means letting the spread run to $280 against you before you act.
  • It does not mean losing twice the trade.

Why it matters

  • Get it the wrong way round and the stop fires far too early, or far too late.
  • A working strategy becomes a losing one without a single rule appearing to change.
  • It is the most common way we see credit spreads mis-sized.
