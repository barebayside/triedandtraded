Exits 13

How a trade ends — and why that decides more than the entry does

  • Does closing at 50% beat holding to expiry?Closing an options trade at half its maximum profit is the most repeated rule in premium selling. Here is what the rule actually claims, and what a test would have to show.
  • Exit 1 — the profit targetA profit target closes an options trade once it has captured a stated fraction of the most it could make. What 50% actually means, and what it costs you.
  • Exit 2 — the stop lossA stop loss on an options trade is a multiple of what you staked, not of what you can lose. Get that backwards and the stop fires far too early or far too late.
  • Exit 3 — the time exitA time exit closes an options trade at a fixed number of days to expiry, in profit or not. It is a rule about gamma wearing a clock’s clothes.
  • Exit 4 — holding to expiryHolding an options trade to expiry is the exit that costs nothing to run and is the control every other exit has to beat.
  • Exit 5 — the technical exitA technical exit closes an options trade when the underlying does something you named in advance. It is the hardest exit to test honestly.
  • Exit 6 — assignmentAssignment ends an options trade by delivering shares. Taken on purpose it is a strategy; taken by surprise it is the most expensive way a defined-risk trade goes wrong.
  • Exit 7 — the rollRolling an options trade closes it and opens a new one further out. It is on the list of exits because so many traders count it as one, and it is the reason losing streaks look flat.
  • The seven ways to exit an options tradeThere are seven ways an options trade can end: profit target, stop loss, time exit, expiry, technical exit, assignment and the roll. What each one does, and what it costs.
  • What is an exit rule?An exit rule is the part of a strategy that decides when a trade ends, stated before the trade opens. Here is what makes one a rule rather than a habit.
  • What is gamma risk near expiry?Gamma risk is how fast your delta changes as the stock moves. Near expiry it grows sharply, which is the reason time exits exist.
  • What is the 21-DTE exit?The 21-DTE exit closes an options trade three weeks before expiry regardless of profit. Where the rule came from, what it is really managing, and what would have to be true for it to work.
  • Why the exit changes the answer more than the entryTwo traders can run identical option entries for a year and reach opposite conclusions. The exit is usually why, and here is the mechanism.

Structures 17

What each strategy is made of, what it pays, and what it costs to be wrong

  • The bear call spreadA bear call spread sells a call above the price and buys a further one above that. The mirror of the bull put spread — and it does not behave like one.
  • The broken-wing butterflyA butterfly with one wing pushed further out, which makes it very cheap to open. What the extra distance buys, and what it costs on the side you moved.
  • The bull put spreadA bull put spread sells a put below the price and buys a further one below that. What it is made of, what it pays, where you actually enter, and the trap that catches people.
  • The calendar spreadA calendar spread sells a near-dated option and buys a longer-dated one at the same strike. A volatility trade wearing a direction trade's clothes.
  • The cash-secured putA cash-secured put is an offer to buy a stock lower, and you are paid for making it. What it pays, what it obliges, and why the cash matters more than the option.
  • The covered callA covered call sells a call against shares you already own. What it pays, what it gives up, and why the income is not the whole story.
  • The debit spreadA debit spread buys one option and sells a cheaper one further out. You pay to open it, and time works against you — the mirror of everything else on this shelf.
  • The diagonal spreadA diagonal spread sells a near-dated option and buys a longer-dated one at a different strike. A calendar and a vertical at the same time.
  • The iron condorAn iron condor sells a put spread and a call spread on the same stock and expiry. What it pays, why its maximum loss is width minus credit and not twice it, and what actually goes wrong.
  • The iron flyAn iron fly sells a straddle at the money and buys wings either side. The largest credit on the shelf, and the narrowest range to keep it in.
  • The naked strangleA naked strangle sells a put below the stock and a call above it, with nothing bought to cap either side. The largest income on the shelf and the only unlimited loss.
  • The poor man's covered callA poor man's covered call replaces the shares in a covered call with a long-dated in-the-money call. What that substitution buys, and what it quietly costs.
  • The ratio spreadA 1x2 ratio spread buys one option and sells two further out. It is a spread with an extra naked leg, and that leg is the whole story.
  • The short straddleA short straddle sells a put and a call at the same strike. The most premium two legs can collect, and the least room to keep it in.
  • What is 0-DTE trading?0-DTE means trading options that expire the same day. It is not a structure — it is a clock, and it changes every structure it is applied to.
  • What is a credit spread?A credit spread sells one option and buys a cheaper one further out, so you are paid to open it and your loss is capped. The parent of the bull put and the bear call.
  • What is the wheel strategy?The wheel sells a cash-secured put, takes assignment, then sells covered calls against the shares. Three positions in a loop — and the loop is where the argument is.

What a strategy is 8

Where an idea stops and something testable starts

  • A default is not an instructionSoftware fills in the numbers you do not choose. Those numbers end up in your results looking exactly like decisions. Ours put one width into more than a hundred thousand trades.
  • Strategy, setup, systemThree words traders use interchangeably that mean different things. A setup is the conditions, a strategy adds the trade and the exit, a system adds the money.
  • What is a backtest?A backtest replays a specified strategy against price history to find out what it would have produced. What it can show, and the four things it cannot fix.
  • What is a specification?A specification is the set of numbers that turns a strategy's name into something two people can run and get the same trades from. For a credit spread there are four.
  • What is a trading strategy?A trading strategy is a rule set complete enough that two people running it on the same data get the same trades. Anything less is an idea with a name on it.
  • What is expectancy?Expectancy is what one trade is worth on average, once the size of the wins and the losses is taken into account. It is the number this site judges a strategy on.
  • Why win rate is the most misleading number in tradingA win rate tells you how often, never how much. A strategy can win 95% of the time and lose money, and the arithmetic that makes it happen is simple.
  • Why “the wheel” is not a strategy yetThe wheel is a sequence of positions, not a strategy. Here is exactly what has to be added before it can be tested, and why two people running it get different answers.
